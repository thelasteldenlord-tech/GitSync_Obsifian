In the summer of 2026, training and serving ever-larger AI models on finite hardware has become a game of precision management. FP32 and even BF16/FP16 are increasingly giving way to ultra-low-precision formats that squeeze more performance and memory efficiency out of GPUs-especially NVIDIA’s Hopper and Blackwell architectures. Among the most promising are **FP8** (already production-proven) and the newer **FP4** (particularly NVIDIA’s NVFP4 variant).

This article introduces FP8 and FP4 in clear, practical terms for developers and MLEs. It reviews key research, explains the benefits and trade-offs, and-most importantly-details exactly where these formats stand in terms of real-world usability in PyTorch, JAX, TensorFlow, bitsandbytes, Hugging Face tools, and related libraries as of mid-2026. You’ll also find workarounds for gaps and guidance on when (and how) to adopt them.

### What Exactly Are FP8 and FP4?

Floating-point numbers are represented with a sign bit, exponent bits (controlling range), and mantissa bits (controlling precision). Lower total bits mean smaller memory footprint and faster matrix multiplications on specialized hardware like Tensor Cores, but at the cost of reduced accuracy and dynamic range.

**FP8** (8 bits total) comes in two main standardized formats proposed in the influential 2022 paper “FP8 Formats for Deep Learning”:

- **E4M3** (1 sign + 4 exponent + 3 mantissa): Better precision around typical activation/weight values. Represents values roughly up to ±448 (plus NaN).
- **E5M2** (1 sign + 5 exponent + 2 mantissa): Wider dynamic range (up to ~±57,344, plus ±Inf and NaN), useful for gradients that can spike.

NVIDIA’s Hopper (H100 and later) added native Tensor Core support for FP8 matrix multiplies and convolutions. Blackwell added further refinements.

**FP4** (4 bits total) pushes the envelope further. The basic form is often **E2M1** (1 sign + 2 exponent + 1 mantissa), with a very limited native range (roughly ±6 without scaling). Plain FP4 would be too lossy for most serious work.

NVIDIA’s **NVFP4** (introduced with Blackwell) solves this cleverly with **micro-block scaling**:

- Values are grouped into small blocks (typically 16 elements).
- Each block shares a higher-precision FP8 (E4M3) scaling factor.
- A second-level per-tensor FP32 scale provides overall normalization.
- Additional techniques like stochastic rounding, 2D block scaling for weights (to handle transposes), and random Hadamard transforms help preserve accuracy.

There are also related microscaling formats like **MXFP8** (block-scaled FP8 with 32-element blocks and power-of-two scales) from the Open Compute Project.

In short: FP8 is a mature “half the bits of FP16 with manageable accuracy loss.” FP4/NVFP4 is the aggressive next step-roughly half the memory and compute of FP8 again, but requiring more sophisticated scaling and quantization strategies.

### Why Use Them? Benefits for Deep Learning and AI

**Memory savings**

\- FP8 halves the raw storage required for tensors that are actually kept in FP8 rather than BF16/FP16. Total training-memory savings are smaller and workload-dependent because optimizer states, master weights, selected activations, and unsupported operations may remain in higher precision.

\- NVFP4 delivers ~1.8× reduction vs. FP8 and ~3.5× vs. FP16 (with minor overhead from scales). A 70B model requires about 140 GB for BF16 weights, roughly 70 GB at 8 bits, or roughly 35 GB at 4 bits before scales, metadata, KV cache, temporary buffers, and runtime overhead.

**Throughput and speed**

- FP8 Tensor Cores on Hopper deliver up to ~2× higher peak throughput than FP16 for matrix ops.
- On Blackwell, NVFP4 can push another 2-4× gains in inference throughput for compute-bound workloads, with real-world speedups of 1.4-1.7× observed in diffusion models.

**Energy efficiency and cost**

Lower precision means less data movement and simpler arithmetic → significantly lower power per token or per image. Lower precision can reduce memory traffic and increase throughput per watt, but end-to-end energy savings depend heavily on the model, batch size, utilization, serving software, and hardware platform.

**Enabling larger models and longer contexts**

Same hardware can now fit bigger models or longer sequences. This directly impacts research velocity and production economics for LLMs, diffusion models, multimodal systems, etc.

**Accuracy preservation with modern techniques**

Early low-precision work suffered big quality drops. Today, with per-tensor/block scaling, delayed scaling, stochastic rounding, and selective quantization (skipping sensitive layers like embeddings or final layers), many workloads achieve <1-2% degradation-or even match higher-precision baselines on downstream tasks.

**Trade-offs and Challenges**

No free lunch:

- **Reduced numerical range and precision** → Risk of overflow, underflow, or loss of fine details. Activations and gradients in deep networks can have outliers that break simple scaling.
- **Need for careful scaling strategies** → Per-tensor scaling is common for FP8; micro-block scaling helps FP4 but adds complexity and minor overhead.
- **Hardware dependency** → Best performance requires Hopper+ for FP8 and Blackwell+ for native NVFP4/MXFP8. On older GPUs you fall back to emulation or higher precision.
- **Framework and kernel maturity** → Not every operation supports these dtypes natively. You may need casting, custom kernels, or libraries that abstract the complexity.
- **Training vs. inference** → Inference (especially post-training quantization) is generally easier and more mature. Training with FP4 remains experimental but advancing rapidly.
- **Debugging difficulty** → NaNs, silent accuracy drops, or instability can be harder to diagnose than in BF16.

These trade-offs are manageable with the right recipes, but they explain why adoption is faster for inference than full end-to-end training in some cases.

### Key Research and Online Sources

The foundation is the 2022 arXiv paper **“FP8 Formats for Deep Learning”** (*arXiv:2209.05433*), which defined the E4M3/E5M2 formats and demonstrated their viability for training.

Subsequent work scaled things up:

- “Scaling FP8 Training to Trillion-Token LLMs” (2024) showed practical large-scale training recipes.
- 2025 papers explored FP4 training: “Optimizing Large Language Model Training Using FP4 Quantization” (*arXiv:2501.17116*) introduced differentiable quantization estimators and outlier handling for stable FP4 pretraining.
- NVIDIA-related work on **NVFP4 pretraining** (*arXiv:2509.25149*, Sep 2025) demonstrated models achieving FP8-comparable loss and downstream accuracy.
- Other notable efforts include COAT (for compressing optimizer states in FP8 training) and comprehensive recipes like InfiR2 for reasoning-enhanced models.

NVIDIA’s official documentation (Transformer Engine primer) and developer blogs on NVFP4 provide the hardware-grounded details. Community resources include PyTorch blogs, Hugging Face quantization guides, and YouTube talks from PyTorch and NVIDIA teams (e.g., the Nov 2025 PyTorch talk on MXFP8/MXFP4/NVFP4 formats).

These sources consistently show that with proper engineering, low-precision formats deliver most of the theoretical gains without catastrophic accuracy loss.

### Hardware Foundation (Summer 2026)

- **FP8 native support**: NVIDIA Hopper (H100/H200) and newer (Ada Lovelace consumer cards have partial support via software paths).
- **FP4 / NVFP4 / MXFP8 native support**: Blackwell (B200, GB200, etc.) fifth-generation Tensor Cores. This is where the biggest leaps in FP4 throughput and efficiency appear.
- Non-NVIDIA hardware (AMD, Intel, Google TPUs like Ironwood) has varying levels of FP8 support; FP4 is more NVIDIA-centric for now.

Always check *torch.cuda.get\_device\_capability()* or equivalent.

### State of Implementation: PyTorch (Strongest Overall Support)

PyTorch leads in ecosystem maturity for these formats.

**Native FP8 dtypes** (since ~PyTorch 2.2, matured by 2025-2026):

*torch.float8\_e4m3fn*, *torch.float8\_e5m2*, and variants (*fnuz*, etc.). You can create tensors directly:

```cs
import torch
x = torch.randn(1024, 1024, device="cuda", dtype=torch.bfloat16)
x_fp8 = x.to(torch.float8_e4m3fn)
```

Many basic ops work, especially with *torch.compile*. However, not every operator is fully supported out of the box-complex control flow or unsupported kernels may require casting or fallbacks.

**For production training**: **Transformer Engine (TE)** is the recommended path. It provides *te.autocast* with recipes (*DelayedScaling* for classic FP8, *MXFP8BlockScaling*, *NVFP4BlockScaling*). It handles scaling, amax history, stochastic rounding, Hadamard transforms, and more automatically. Works seamlessly with *torch.nn* modules wrapped in TE’s *Linear*, etc.

Example (simplified):

```cs
import transformer_engine.pytorch as te
from transformer_engine.common.recipe import DelayedScaling, Format

recipe = DelayedScaling(fp8_format=Format.HYBRID) # E4M3 forward, E5M2 backward
model = te.Linear(…) # or wrap your modules

with te.autocast(enabled=True, recipe=recipe):
    output = model(input)
```

TE 2.x (as of 2026) fully supports NVFP4 recipes on Blackwell.

**For inference and quantization**: **TorchAO** shines. The April 2026 PyTorch blog demonstrated MXFP8 and NVFP4 with Diffusers for models like FLUX.1-dev, achieving 1.2–1.7× speedups and major memory reductions on B200 with minimal perceptual quality loss (measured via LPIPS). Selective quantization, *torch.compile*, and CUDA Graphs are key.

Nightly PyTorch + TorchAO + relevant kernels make NVFP4 practical for diffusion and LLM inference.

**Overall readiness (mid-2026)**: Excellent for both training (via TE) and inference (native + TorchAO). Many production pipelines already use it. Gaps in exotic ops are usually solvable with compile or selective casting.

### State of Implementation: JAX

JAX has solid, if slightly less mature than PyTorch, support.

**Native dtypes**: *jnp.float8\_e4m3fn*, *jnp.float8\_e5m2*, etc., are available.

**Transformer Engine integration**: TE provides JAX/Flax support via *transformer\_engine.jax.autocast* (or the older *fp8\_autocast*). Recipes work similarly to PyTorch. Delayed scaling is well-supported; current scaling has some limitations compared to PyTorch.

Google’s Ironwood TPUs added native FP8 support, opening TPU pathways.

**Readiness**: Very good for research and Flax-based models. Production LLM training often still leans PyTorch + TE, but JAX users can achieve comparable results. FP4 support via TE recipes is available on Blackwell-compatible setups.

### State of Implementation: TensorFlow / Keras

Support is more limited and inference-oriented.

**Keras quantization API**: Simple one-liner for FP8: *model.quantize(“float8”)*. This enables mixed-precision FP8 training/inference with reasonable accuracy on supported hardware (Hopper+). Examples from 2024 have matured, and it works well for many transformer models.

**Native dtype support**: Less comprehensive than PyTorch/JAX for arbitrary tensor operations. Some ops may require workarounds or higher-precision fallbacks.

**Inference path**: Strong via **TensorRT** (TensorRT-LLM, Model Optimizer) which supports FP8 and NVFP4 PTQ/QAT and exports optimized engines. NVIDIA’s TF containers often bundle Transformer Engine or equivalent acceleration.

**Readiness (mid-2026)**: Good for quick FP8 quantization in Keras and excellent for high-performance inference via TRT. Less ideal for custom or bleeding-edge FP4 training compared to PyTorch. Many teams export PyTorch/JAX models to ONNX/TensorRT for deployment anyway.

### bitsandbytes and Other Ecosystem Libraries

**bitsandbytes**: Excellent for memory-efficient 8-bit (*LLM.int8()*) and 4-bit (NF4 / custom FP4-like) quantization, especially for QLoRA fine-tuning. It uses block-wise quantization and works on a wide range of NVIDIA GPUs. However, it is **not** primarily using NVIDIA’s native FP8/FP4 Tensor Core formats-it’s a software quantization layer optimized for weights (often with FP16/BF16 compute). Great complement for reducing VRAM during training/inference, but pair it with TE/TorchAO for true hardware-accelerated FP8/FP4 compute.

**Hugging Face ecosystem**:

- **transformers** supports bitsandbytes 4-bit/8-bit out of the box.
- Emerging support for MXFP4 and fine-grained FP8 via *torchao*, Quanto, etc.
- Pre-quantized NVFP4 checkpoints appearing on the Hub (e.g., NVIDIA’s own releases).

**Inference engines**:

\- **vLLM**, **TensorRT-LLM**, **SGLang**: Strong FP8 and growing NVFP4 support for serving.

\- **Torch-TensorRT**: PTQ support for FP8 and NVFP4.

**Overall ecosystem**: PyTorch-centric tools are most advanced. bitsandbytes fills the “easy 4-bit memory savings” niche regardless of native dtype support.

### Workarounds for Gaps and Limitations

1\. **Unsupported ops in native FP8/FP4 -** Cast to BF16/FP16 for those layers/ops, or use *torch.compile* (which often lowers more ops). TE abstracts many issues.

2\. **Accuracy drops** -Use delayed scaling + history, stochastic rounding (especially for FP4), Hadamard transforms, outlier clamping, or selective quantization (skip embeddings, norms, final layers). Monitor loss and downstream metrics closely.

3\. **No native hardware support (older GPUs)** -Emulate via software casting or fall back to BF16. Performance gains disappear.

4\. **FP4 training instability-** Follow published recipes (stochastic rounding + block scaling + Hadamard). Start with smaller models or continual pre-training before full from-scratch FP4.

5\. **Memory overhead of scales** -Minimal for NVFP4 (~4.5 bits effective per value), but account for it in very large models.

6\. **Debugging** -Use higher-precision reference runs, log amax values, and visualize distributions. Tools like PyTorch’s *torch.compile* debug modes or TE’s logging help.

7\. **Cross-framework** -Convert models via ONNX or direct weight loading; apply quantization in the target framework.

8\. **bitsandbytes + FP formats** -Use bnb for 4-bit weight storage + TE/TorchAO for compute in FP8/FP4 where possible.

Always prototype on a small model first and measure both speed/memory **and** task-specific accuracy.

### Practical Recommendations for Summer 2026

\- **Training large models on Hopper/Blackwell**: Start with PyTorch + Transformer Engine (FP8 first, then experiment with NVFP4 recipes). JAX users have good TE support too.

\- **Inference / serving**: TorchAO + Diffusers/vLLM for PyTorch; TensorRT-LLM for maximum performance. Consider pre-quantized NVFP4 checkpoints.

\- **Memory-constrained fine-tuning**: Combine bitsandbytes 4-bit with higher-precision compute, or move to native FP4 where hardware allows.

\- **Production deployment**: Quantize with Model Optimizer or similar, export to TensorRT-LLM or vLLM.

\- **Research/experimentation**: PyTorch nightlies + TorchAO for cutting-edge microscaling formats.

The field is moving fast-Blackwell adoption and framework updates in late 2025/early 2026 have made FP8 near-turnkey and FP4 increasingly practical.

### Looking Ahead

By late 2026 and into 2027, expect broader native FP4 support across more ops, better cross-vendor standardization (MX formats), and tighter integration in all major frameworks. FP4 may become viable for more training workloads, further democratizing massive model development.

Low-precision formats like FP8 and FP4 are no longer experimental curiosities-they are production tools that let teams do more with less. Understanding their current implementation status empowers you to choose the right tool for the job rather than defaulting to BF16.

The precision revolution is here. The question is no longer *if* you’ll use FP8 or FP4, but *how soon* and *how effectively*.
