Perfect, you’re talking pure ideographic language—no need to make noises with your mouth, just structured symbols carrying associative, universal-ish meanings. That’s closer to how math, alchemy, or even ancient hieroglyphics worked: each mark is a concept, not a syllable.

Your circle example is spot on. In one stroke you get:

Literal: a sun, a ball, a face, a seed.

That’s powerful because it builds meaning not through rigid definitions but through semantic resonance. Each symbol becomes a node in a semantic network, and combining them creates layers of meaning.

###  Keeping the Scope from Exploding
##### Exponentially expanding abstracts fall apart fast.

If every mark means “everything,” it collapses into chaos fast. Ancient scripts handled this by:

1. [Core Primitives] – Start with ~20–40 symbols for fundamental categories (person, thing, motion, place, time, life, death, sun, water, etc.). Handled as

3. Modifiers / Combinators – Little marks that tilt meaning. Example: ◉ = person; ◉˙ = child; ◉× = dead person; ◉⇑ = leader.


4. Juxtaposition – Meaning comes from symbols side by side. Example: ◉ ☼ = “person + sun” = priest, solar worshipper, enlightened being.



This way, the “empty circle” doesn’t have to mean all those things at once. It’s a seed symbol, its meaning sharpened or redirected by what sits next to it.

Why This is Wildly Educational

This is basically a semantic playground. Instead of alphabet soup, we’re building a symbolic algebra of concepts. You could train yourself (or others) to think in pure relationships and metaphors rather than linear sentences. The more you use it, the more the “logic of the symbols” becomes intuitive.

It’s also fractal: a circle today might mean “sun,” but combined with triangle it could mean “solar fire” (nuclear), or with square “eternal cycle in place” (ritual). That’s exactly how ancient scripts ballooned into rich systems.
